Pensacola History

Pensacola in the 18th Century

A hurricane drove an entire town off Santa Rosa Island. A generation later, the American Revolution reached the bluffs above Pensacola Bay. Between those events, Pensacola went from a Spanish military settlement to the capital of British West Florida. By the end of the century, it was Spanish again, and its harbor connected merchants and Indigenous trading towns across the Gulf South.2, 5, 6, 10

Spain’s settlements around the bay

Spain established Presidio Santa María de Galve in 1698 to guard Pensacola Bay as France expanded along the Gulf Coast. A presidio was a fortified settlement with a military garrison. This one answered to New Spain and received its supplies chiefly through Veracruz. The French took the post in 1719 and burned it. Spain returned to the bay in 1722 and built a new settlement on the sheltered north side of Santa Rosa Island.1, 2

A hurricane destroyed the island settlement in 1752. In the years that followed, the Spanish moved their town to the mainland, where they built Presidio San Miguel de Panzacola in what is now downtown Pensacola. Archaeological work puts everyday life back into that story. Near the old fort gate, University of West Florida researchers uncovered a building they interpret as a possible ground-floor trading post with living quarters above. The building came down when the British expanded the fort in 1767.2, 3, 4

The capital of British West Florida

At the end of the Seven Years’ War in 1763, Spain ceded Florida to Britain and recovered Havana, which British forces had captured the previous year. Britain divided Florida into two provinces and made Pensacola the capital of West Florida. The province stretched along the Gulf from the Apalachicola River to the Mississippi, taking in parts of present-day Alabama and Mississippi. Pensacola now housed a provincial government alongside the garrison.5, 6

British surveyor Elias Durnford laid out a street grid with military ground at its center, houses along the adjoining streets, and garden plots farther out. That grid helped shape today’s downtown. During the Revolution, West Florida also drew Loyalists fleeing conflict elsewhere. Thomas Kitchin’s 1781 map gives a period view of the province and includes an inset of Pensacola, so readers can compare the town’s layout with the larger territory it administered.6, 7

The siege of 1781

Spain entered the war against Britain in 1779. After taking British posts on the lower Mississippi and capturing Mobile, Spanish commander Bernardo de Gálvez turned to Pensacola. The campaign began in March 1781. British defenses included Fort George and two outworks on the ridge above town. That high ground mattered because it commanded the approaches to Pensacola from inland.5, 8, 12

On May 8, a Spanish shell set off the powder magazine at the Queen’s Redoubt. Gálvez’s campaign diary describes the blast and his troops’ occupation of the ruined position. British governor Peter Chester and military commander John Campbell agreed to terms, and the formal surrender followed on May 10. Spain took Pensacola in 1781. The 1783 peace settlement confirmed Spain’s recovery of both East and West Florida.8, 9, 5

A Spanish port tied to the interior

Pensacola became the headquarters of Panton, Leslie and Company, a merchant firm with trading posts across the Southeast. Under Spanish rule, the company traded with Indigenous communities, including Creek towns, and moved goods between the coast and the interior. That business depended on enslaved labor. Historian Clinton Hough documents people brought to Pensacola to work at the company’s warehouse, as well as a company ship that arrived in 1790 carrying trade goods and nine enslaved Africans.10, 11

By the end of the century, Spanish authorities were also rebuilding defenses at the entrance to the bay. The geography helps explain the choices made across the century. A force at the entrance could control access by sea, the waterfront could receive supplies and commercial cargo, and the ridge above town could protect its landward approaches. Historic Pensacola’s archaeological trail shows where the downtown fort stood and locates Fort George separately, uphill. Walking between those sites makes the difference between defending the waterfront and holding the heights easier to see.12, 4
