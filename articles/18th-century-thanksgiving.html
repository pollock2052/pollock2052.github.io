Thanksgiving in the 18th Century

By Brandon Pollock · Published November 26, 2026

On December 18, 1777, a Continental soldier in Pennsylvania remembered being served rice and vinegar for Thanksgiving. Twelve years later, George Washington called for a thanksgiving on November 26. Both observances asked Americans to give thanks together, but the date, the occasion, and even what people ate depended on where they were and who called the day.

When a governor named the day

Before independence, colonial governors could set a day for public thanksgiving. In Massachusetts, Governor Francis Bernard appointed November 27 in 1766 and December 1 in 1768. Both dates were Thursdays. The surviving proclamations show how familiar an autumn thanksgiving already was in Massachusetts and how readily its date could shift from one year to the next.1, 2

Congress calls for thanksgiving and prayer

In November 1777, the Continental Congress recommended that the states set aside Thursday, December 18, for public thanksgiving. The proclamation thanked God for providing for the troops and for recent military success. It also called for confession of sins, asked for forgiveness through Jesus Christ, and prayed for independence and peace. Its concerns reached beyond the army. Congress asked for blessings on farmers, trade, and schools as well.3

For Continental soldier Joseph Plumb Martin, the day was memorable for a different reason. In his later memoir, he recalled a ration of half a gill of rice and a tablespoonful of vinegar per man. The proclamation tells us what Congress hoped Americans would do. Martin’s account tells us what one soldier remembered eating in camp.4

Washington’s November 26

At the request of the first federal Congress, President George Washington designated Thursday, November 26, 1789, as a day of thanksgiving and prayer. He asked Americans to thank God for protection during the war, for civil and religious liberty, and for the peaceful establishment of the new government. He also asked them to seek forgiveness and pray that public officials would govern wisely. The proclamation appointed a day for 1789. The nationwide rule placing Thanksgiving on the fourth Thursday in November came in 1941.5, 6

What might have been on the table?

Amelia Simmons’s American Cookery, published in Hartford in 1796, includes two recipes under “Pompkin.” Both bake a pumpkin-and-egg filling in pastry. One uses cream, sugar, mace, nutmeg, and ginger. The other uses milk, molasses, allspice, and ginger. Those recipes offer a starting point for trying a late-18th-century pumpkin dish at home. Martin’s rice and vinegar is the meal we can actually place at a particular Thanksgiving.7
