The 18th-Century Linen Shirt

A shirt could have a body cut almost straight from the loom and still allow room to reach, bend, and work. The shape came from where the maker added cloth, where they gathered it, and where they reinforced it. Surviving American and European shirts show how those choices worked.12

What the shirt was used for

In the garments examined here, the shirt was the first layer a man or boy put on. A coat and waistcoat could cover most of it, though the collar, cuffs, and any ruffles could still show. The Met’s notes on a period waistcoat also give an example of when shirtsleeves could be seen in public. Men could play cricket or bowl without their coats.4

A white shirt took work to keep white. Colonial Williamsburg describes regular washing and the use of starch and bluing to maintain the look of its surviving examples. The English ruffled shirt also bears a small cross-stitched mark near the hip, a detail easily missed when looking only at its collar and cuffs.3

How straight pieces made room to move

Start with the body and sleeves. A surviving British shirt at Colonial Williamsburg has a full, straight body drawn into the collar with fine gathers. Its sleeves are pleated into the shirt body, with much of that fullness concentrated at the top of the arm. Small pieces at the base of the neck help shape the opening, and narrow strips reinforce the shoulders. The catalog records a selvage-to-selvage cloth width of 29 inches for that shirt. That’s a useful measurement for studying this particular garment.3

At the armpit, a gusset gives the sleeve room to move. There was more than one way to make it. A Massachusetts shirt dated 1750–1770 has its underarm gussets cut as part of the sleeve pieces. The Met’s French shirt, dated about 1780, has piecing that adds fullness in front while reducing bulk at the waist. Both examples are worth studying before assuming every maker used the same cutting plan.1

What the cuffs tell you

Look closely at the wristbands before deciding how a shirt fastened. Colonial Williamsburg’s plain Massachusetts example has wristbands made for sleeve buttons, the period equivalent of cufflinks. The British ruffled example also has paired buttonholes at each wrist. Its bands measure just half an inch wide, while the finer linen ruffles extend about two inches. Those are two separate measurements, easy to confuse when you first look at a photo.3

Choosing a shirt to reproduce

Choose a surviving example close to the place and date you want to portray. Compare the collar height, wrist closures, sleeve fullness, and shoulder reinforcement before buying fabric or cutting a pattern. The plain Massachusetts shirt and the British ruffled shirt are good starting points because their museum records describe how the pieces join. The listings also give you something a photograph alone can’t supply, including cloth width where recorded and the construction of parts hidden inside the garment.13
